NeurIPS 2026 EEG/EMG Foundation Challenge · Track 2, BCI decoding

I let AI agents run my EEG competition entry for 48 days.

I have worked on EEG foundation models for years: networks pre-trained on many people's brain recordings and then adapted to a new task. In October I entered one, ST-EEGFormer, which I co-authored, in a brain–computer interface competition and let Claude agents do the work. I typed instructions and made the uploads; they did the rest. The agents and I finished 13th of 302. Not so great. Maybe that is as far as these models go on this task. More likely, my supervision was the weak part. The code, the decision log, the prompts that changed things and the mistakes are in the repository.

The score is accuracy averaged over the three classes (balanced accuracy) on a test set we never saw; guessing gives 0.333. I read the place off the live leaderboard when it closed. The public page lists only the top ten.

The task

Work out which of three things a person is doing in their head (imagined movement, mental arithmetic or word association) from four seconds of EEG, 41 electrodes at 120 samples a second. The test covers ten people the model has earlier recordings of and ten it has never seen. It gets one window at a time and is never told who the person is or whether it was right.

How the agents were organised

It was not one agent. It was a small team of Claude sessions with fixed roles: a manager, one worker for each competition track, and in the last days a reviewer. I typed. They read the rules, wrote the code, queued the jobs, checked each other's work and built the submission files. I uploaded each file myself; the agents were never allowed to.

Me
I set the goals, asked questions, and approved or refused what the agents proposed. I made every upload.
Manager agent
Ran no experiments. Kept the plan and the list of what waited on me, checked in every hour, read the leaderboard, passed findings between tracks and called the panels.
Worker agents
One per competition track: decoding images from EEG, BCI decoding, and predicting sleep onset. Each kept its own decision log. The Track 2 worker wrote and ran all the code behind this page.
Review agent
Added for the last four days. Recomputed results independently and read each change before a file went out.
Helper agents
Short-lived. A worker or the manager started one for a single bounded job: read a long log, check a build, review a change with fresh eyes.
Expert panels
Called for important decisions and code. Each worker wrote a short brief. A model from another company and agents with no memory of our plan were asked to attack it. The manager wrote up the conclusion.

Three rules held this together. A worker writes down what result would count as a pass before it runs the experiment. Nothing becomes an upload without a line I type in that worker's own session. Every candidate is scored as the packaged file, the way the organisers would run it. The rules were not always kept, and the lapses are in the log.

Over the whole challenge that came to about 300 helper agents, 25 panel rounds and 140 written reviews.

The foundation model was level with the best small network, or slightly ahead.

FBCNet, a filter-bank convolutional network, read 0.742. One fine-tuned ST-EEGFormer network read 0.739 after 30 training epochs and 0.757 after 100, and three of them voting read 0.774. The two are not scored in the same form: FBCNet is taken as it sat in the ensemble, with per-person copies, and the ST-EEGFormer networks have none. In a later check on ten new people, six ST-EEGFormer networks together read 0.703, against 0.678 for FBCNet, 0.648 for IFNet and 0.637 for ShallowNet (three runs each).

The other foundation models did not get there.

MOR, an in-house model, came closest at 0.727 with three networks voting. REVE, LaBraM and CBraMod stayed between 0.48 and 0.58. Most of these got one configuration and little tuning, so this says they were not competitive as we tried them. It is not a ranking of the models.

Classical pipelines were far behind.

The best, a filter-bank Riemannian classifier with logistic regression, read 0.585. Filter-bank CSP with LDA read 0.536.

A model's score alone did not decide whether it helped.

Adding ST-EEGFormer to the October ensemble was worth 0.017. Adding MOR was worth 0.0005. A small tree model that read only 0.469 alone on ten new people added 0.012 to the ensemble, because its errors were almost uncorrelated with the rest.

By size, the final entry was almost all foundation model.

It held 521 networks and three small tree models, about 1.84 billion parameters in all. The six ST-EEGFormer networks hold 98.9% of them. The 495 small convolutional networks together hold 18 million.

One caveat works in the foundation model's favour: its extra pre-training had seen unlabelled recordings of three of these four people. The convolutional networks had never seen two of them. The ten-person check used the released weights without that step. All 38 single-model results, the other test setups and the caveats are in the model comparison.

What I think we learned

This is one entry in one competition. Read these as observations, not findings. "Our own validation" means data we held back ourselves, not the official test.

The foundation model helped, but not enough.

In early November, taking the fine-tuned ST-EEGFormer networks out of the ensemble cost about 0.036 on our own validation. The largest single gain of the last week was a better data-augmentation setup for them. A smaller version that we pre-trained from scratch on open data only was 0.12 behind and unusable. (The published checkpoint we started from was not pre-trained on open data only.)

The biggest gain came from a fact about the test, not from a model.

Every recording contains the three tasks equally often. Correcting the predictions as they come in, so that they stay balanced, was worth about 0.12 on our own validation: 0.07 on recordings that were already easy and 0.15 on the hard ones. Refitting the foundation model's small output heads on our own predictions during the test helped a little (about 0.003). Changing its network weights during the test never helped where we tried it.

The features look good enough. What is missing is knowledge of the person.

As a check, we fitted a simple classifier on the foundation model's features using a new person's own earlier windows and the true answers, which the real test never gives. It beat our whole system by 0.040. With our own predictions in place of the true answers, it lost 0.064.

Less than half of the measured gain survived the hidden test.

For the one step we could measure, the gain on our own validation showed up at 37 to 48% of its size in the official score. The last candidate, a small gain by our measure, scored 0.006 lower than the file it was built on.

What went wrong

The expensive mistakes were errors of judgement under time pressure, not bugs.

Our validation did not train the way the submitted model did.

Until 19 November the validation runs trained on one recording session per person, while the submitted models trained on all 14 public sessions, where two people hold 43% of the data. A panel found the mismatch two days before the close.

The agents decided the metric was wrong.

One agent argued that our validation metric was biased, and it was re-weighted. A second-opinion review asked for one check. The argument failed it and was withdrawn the same day.

They judged our chances from an old leaderboard.

The public page was two days out of date. We thought third place stood at 0.848. It was already 0.862.

I asked for every small gain to be included.

The rule the agents wrote from that sentence had no separate check for people the model had seen before. The last candidate passed it and scored 0.838, which is 0.006 below the file it was built on.

"now, any small gain is plausible and should be included"Me to the manager agent, 20 November, 16:30

The milestone page shows which of my prompts changed the direction, and whether that helped. Why 13th and not higher? It could be the foundation model, the agents, or me. One run cannot separate the three. My guess is me.

What would you have done differently?

The competition is closed, but two questions are still open for me. Can an EEG foundation model do better on this task than we managed? And was this a good way to run AI agents on a research problem: one manager, one worker per track, panels for the big decisions, rules written before results, and me typing a few lines a day?

I would like to hear ideas on either one: the model, the agent set-up, the workflow, or the way I supervised. The code and the full decision log are in the repository. The data and the trained weights are not; the data comes from the organisers. Open an issue, or run it yourself. I will test the model ideas that look most promising when I have spare time and GPUs, and log what happens in the same format, including when it fails.
